The Introduction:
The purpose of this greenhouse/nursery project is to integrate the data allocated from three IoT devices used inside a greenhouse. The IoT devices are; soil sensors, a weather station, and a water controller. All of those devices collect different information about the condition of the greenhouse. The soil sensors measure soil moisture and the temperature, the weather station records the environmental conditions, and the water controller logs the irrigation events. After combining all of these sources into one whole dataset, the greenhouse staff can thoroughlt examine how soil conditions, weather, and irrigation activity relate to one another.The integrated dataset is stored integrated_greenhouse.csv and includes a sample of readings from July 15, 2026, spanning from 6:00 AM to 12:00 PM.
The Three Data Sources:
The greenhouse project uses three IoT data sources: the soil sensors, a weather station, and a water controller. Each of those devices collect different information and stores it in a different file format. The soil sensor data is stored in batch102.csv, which uutlizes the CSV data format. Every row represents a reading from one soil sensor at a particular date as well as a particular time. The file contains the sensor ID, greenhouse bed ID, soil moisture measured as volumetric water content abbreviated as (VWC), and soil temperature measured in Fahrenheit. A BatteryLevel column was added, but the values remain blank because battery the measurements were not provided. The second source of data is the weather station, which stores its data in feed102.xml utilizing the XML data format. Each observation represents different environmental conditions at a specific date and time. The weather station measures air temperature in Celsius, humidity as a percentage, and wind speed in miles per hour. A sunlight field was also added, but its values remain blank because sunlight measurements were not included within the original data. The third and final data source is the water controller, which stores irrigation activity in stream102.json utilizng the JSON data format. Each entry in the log array represents an irrigation event at a specific date and time. The records include the irrigation zone, the water flow rate in gallons per minute (GPM), and the pump status, indicating whether the pump is turned on or off. An activationMode field was added, but its values remain null because that information was also unavailable. Although the three devices monitor the same greenhouse, they collect different measurements and do not always record information at matching times. When combining their data together, it makes it a little easier to examine the relationships between the soil conditions, environmental measurements, and irrigation activity.
Questions the Data can Answer:
When combining the three greenhouse data sources,it makes it possible to investigate different questions that one device alone cannot answer itself. A good example would be, a greenhouse staff could examine whether soil moisture decreases when air temperature increases! This requires comparing measurements from the soil sensors and the weather station. Another good question would be whether the irrigation events are followed by an increase in soil moisture. The water controller shows when irrigation activity occurs, while the soil sensors show how moisture levels change over time. The combined data could also help staff determine whether high temperatures and low humidity occur during periods when the soil is becoming drier. This information could help the staff make better decisions about the greenhouse monitoring as well as the watering schedules. However, the data does not identify which irrigation zones correspond to specific greenhouse beds, so the relationship between an irrigation event and a particular bed can't necessarily be confirmed.
Why I chose CSV file format:
I chose the CSV file format because, in my opinion, it is one of the easiest formats to organize and understand. It allows someone looking at the data to clearly see the different measurements and interpret the information more easily compared to JSON or XML files. Another reason I chose CSV is that it organizes information into rows and columns, which makes it easier to compare data from the three greenhouse devices. CSV files can also be opened in Excel, which makes them convenient for reviewing and analyzing information.
The Integrated Data Records/Fields:
Each row in my integrated greenhouse CSV represents a soil sensor reading taken at a specified date and time. I used the soil sensor data as the starting point because it identifies the individual sensors and the greenhouse beds. I then matched the soil readings with weather station observations recorded at the same date and time. This allowed me to include soil moisture, soil temperature, air temperature, humidity, and the wind speed in the same record. I also included water controller information when an irrigation event occurred at the exact same time. An example wwould be at 6:00 AM, the water controller recorded an event for Zone 2 with a flow rate of 4.04 gallons per minute and the pump turned on. When there was no matching water controller event, I left those fields blank. The integrated dataset contains 15 fields covering the date, time, sensor and bed identification, soil measurements, weather conditions, and irrigation activity. The data dictionary explains the meaning, data type, and units of each field. Although the records can be matched by date and time, the data does not establish which irrigation zones correspond to the specific greenhouse beds.
The Data Cleaning:
Before I started combining the three greenhouse data sources, I had to make sure their information was organized consistently. I think pne of the main differences was how the files recorded dates and times. The soil sensor and water controller files used the MM/DD/YYYY date format, while the weather station used DD/MM/YYYY. I standardized the dates to MM/DD/YYYY in my integrated CSV file. I also changed the times to a 24-hour format so the readings would be easier to match! A solid example would be 6:00 AM became 06:00, and 12:00 PM became 12:00. Another part of the data cleaning process involved missing values. The BatteryLevel, Sunlight, and ActivationMode fields were added to the original source files, but their measurements were not provided. Instead of making up fake values, I left those fields blank in the integrated dataset.
The Samples, Benefits, and the Limitations: 
In my integrated dataset, I selected greenhouse readings from July 15, 2026, between 6:00 AM and 12:00 PM. I chose this six-hour period because it contained matching soil sensor and weather station readings, along with a water controller event at 6:00 AM. I selected a smaller sample instead of using every available record to make the integration process easier to manage. I noticed that combining the three sources makes it easier to compare soil moisture, soil temperature, air temperature, humidity, and irrigation activity in one place. This information could help the greenhouse staff recognize patterns and make better decisions about watering and monitoring the greenhouse However, the devices record information at different intervals, so their timestamps do not always match. Some water controller fields are blank because no event occurred at the exact time of the soil readings. Also, the dataset does not identify which irrigation zones correspond to specific greenhouse beds, so I cannot confirm that a particular irrigation event watered a specificed bed. Overall, I believe that combining the data provides a clearer picture of greenhouse conditions, but some details from the original files are lost when everything is organized into a single CSV file.
